We conducted training across dual NVIDIA T4 GPUs (16~GB GDDR6 VRAM per card, 32~GB aggregate) under PyTorch DistributedDataParallel (DDP) using FP16 Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP). Model scaling spans three parameter tiers: Nebium-Small (117M parameters, $L=12, d_{\text{model}}=768, H=12$), Nebium-Medium (345M, $L=24, d_{\text{model}}=1024, H=16$), and Nebium-Large (762M, $L=36, d_{\text{model}}=1280, H=20$).

Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_hardware}(a) and (b) report training throughput and memory residency across model tiers. Nebium-Small processes 14,250 tokens per second using 4,850~MB VRAM per GPU. Nebium-Medium achieves 9,450 tokens per second at 9,250~MB. Nebium-Large uses 8-bit AdamW and activation checkpointing to reach 4,650 tokens per second while holding peak memory to 14,450~MB, within the 16~GB board capacity. All models train with AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.95$, weight decay $\lambda=0.1$); setting $\beta_2=0.95$ dampens variance across sparse coordinate tokens. Optimization follows a 2,000-step linear warmup to $\eta_{\text{max}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}$, decaying via cosine annealing to $\eta_{\text{min}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-5}$ over 50,000 steps with gradient clipping at $\|\mathbf{g}\|_2 \le 1.0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_hardware}(c)).

\end{figure}

Autoregressive token training follows the compute-optimal scaling framework of \textcite{hoffmann2022training}, where compute FLOPs scale as $C \approx 6ND$ and the optimal token budget satisfies $D^* = 20N$. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_scaling_laws}, Nebium-Small requires 2.3 billion tokens, Nebium-Medium requires 6.9 billion, and Nebium-Large scales to 15.2 billion tokens.

Empirical loss convergence across 20 epochs exhibits three distinct regimes. In the initial syntax acquisition phase (epochs 1--4), cross-entropy loss drops steeply as attention heads learn legal UCI string syntax and high-frequency opening plies. During the tactical alignment phase (epochs 5--14), the model resolves piece coordination and central exchange structures, producing steady loss reduction. In the final phase (epochs 15--20), convergence slows toward an empirical entropy floor of 2.7278 nats. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_training_dynamics}, Nebium-Medium reaches a training loss of 2.6932 nats at epoch 19, with held-out validation loss converging to 1.88 nats (matching a validation perplexity of 6.55). Increasing transformer depth ($L=24$) yielded higher compute efficiency per FLOP than widening shallow configurations at equal parameter counts.

Evaluating autoregressive generation without external chess engine constraints reveals how internal attention heads learn move legality over training. Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_accuracy_legality} tracks prediction accuracy and raw legality over 20 epochs. Top-1 move prediction starts at 14.8\% and climbs to 29.4\%, while top-5 accuracy improves from 38.6\% to 82.3\%, showing that plausible continuations consistently sit near the top of the predicted distribution.

Raw move legality over 20-move sequences reaches 99.6\% by epoch 20. However, open-ended rollouts show that legality depends on game horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:puzzle_generation_benchmarks}). Move legality stays at 99.8\% through the opening (plies 1--20) where standard book lines guide choices. In the middlegame (plies 21--60), legality drops to 84.1\% as piece interactions grow dense and tactical pins require broader context. In endgames (plies 61+), legality falls to 63.5\% without intervention. Common illegalities include moving pinned pieces, stepping kings into check, and path collisions, all of which dynamic logit masking resolves during play.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} compares Nebium against statistical and transformer baselines on identical validation splits. The 3-gram Markov model achieves a validation loss of 4.82 and perplexity of 123.96, with top-1 accuracy limited to 14.2\%. Because n-gram transitions cannot condition on history beyond two plies, the statistical baseline fails whenever piece interactions depend on distant opening developments.

The vanilla GPT-2 baseline (28.1M parameters with learned absolute positional embeddings, Post-LayerNorm, and GELU) attains a validation loss of 2.14 (perplexity 8.50) and top-1 accuracy of 44.8\%. Nebium-Small (117M) improves top-1 accuracy to 48.2\% and lowers perplexity to 7.46, while Nebium-Medium (345M) achieves 51.4\% top-1 accuracy and 6.55 perplexity. Component attribution confirms the source of these gains. Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) contribute a 3.4\% improvement in top-1 accuracy by preserving relative ply offsets across coordinate pairs. SwiGLU gating reduces cross-entropy loss by 0.13 nats compared to standard GELU, and pre-RMSNorm prevents gradient norm spikes during FP16 execution without requiring aggressive gradient clipping.

\subsection{Qualitative Move Analysis and Autoregressive Trajectory Dynamics}
\label{sec:exp_qualitative}
\label{sec:exp_stockfish}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/italian_game_qualitative.pdf}
  \caption[Qualitative Board Trajectory: Italian Game and King Coordinate Drift]{Qualitative rollout from the starting board: (a) sound opening development through 8.~b4 Bb6; (b) central pawn tension after 11.~c3 attacking Black's bishop on d4; (c) failure mode where Black plays 11\dots~Kh8?!, conceding the bishop to 12.~cxd4 followed by repetitive king oscillation.}
  \label{fig:italian_qualitative}
\end{figure}

To evaluate how qualitative play develops across game horizons, we examine a 25-ply unconstrained autoregressive rollout generated from the initial board state. The opening follows the Italian Game (\textit{Giuoco Pianissimo}):
\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Bc5 4. O-O Nf6 5. d3 O-O} \\
\texttt{6. Nc3 d6 7. a3 h6 8. b4 Bb6 9. Nd5 Nxd4 10. Nxd4 Bxd4 11. c3}
\end{center}

Through the first 11 moves (plies 1--21), generation closely mirrors standard opening practice (Figure~\ref{fig:italian_qualitative}(a) and (b)). Both sides develop minor pieces toward the center (knights to \texttt{c3}, \texttt{f3}, \texttt{c6}, \texttt{f6}; bishops to \texttt{c4} and \texttt{c5}), castle kingside on moves 4 and 5, and play prophylactic pawn advances (\texttt{7. a3}, \texttt{7... h6}). White expands on the queenside with \texttt{8. b4 Bb6}, exchanges knights on \texttt{d5}, and strikes centrally with \texttt{11. c3}, attacking Black's bishop on \texttt{d4}.

At ply 21, competitive play requires Black to resolve the direct threat on \texttt{d4}, with standard theory prescribing \texttt{11... Bb6} or \texttt{11... Nxd5 12. exd5 Bb6}. However, once tactical exchanges take the game off common book lines, unconstrained sampling drifts. Instead of safeguarding the bishop, the model plays an unprovoked king move (\texttt{11... Kh8?!}), abandoning the bishop on \texttt{d4} (Figure~\ref{fig:italian_qualitative}(c)). White captures the piece with \texttt{12. cxd4}, establishing a decisive material advantage. Over subsequent plies, the model exhibits repetitive back-rank king oscillation (\texttt{12... Kh7}, \texttt{13... Kg8}), shuffling between adjacent squares without strategic purpose.

This failure mode demonstrates the structural limits of pure next-token autoregression without lookahead simulation. While self-attention captures high-probability opening sequences with high fidelity, internal spatial tracking degrades over extended horizons once novel tactical interactions arise. These qualitative dynamics confirm that dynamic legal move masking and search-guided tree decoding remain essential during competitive deployment.
