\section{Experimental Results and Empirical Analysis}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Training Configurations, Model Variants, and Hardware Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}
\label{sec:exp_latency}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/hardware_and_optimization_hq.pdf}
  \caption[Hardware Throughput, Memory Residency, and Learning Schedule]{Hardware throughput and optimization dynamics on dual NVIDIA T4 GPUs: (a) token processing speed across model sizes; (b) peak VRAM usage against the 16~GB physical ceiling; (c) learning rate warmup and cosine decay schedule.}
  \label{fig:wandb_hardware}
\end{figure}

Training was performed on dual NVIDIA T4 GPUs (16~GB GDDR6 VRAM per card, 32~GB aggregate) using PyTorch DistributedDataParallel (DDP) with FP16 Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP). The model family spans three parameter tiers: Nebium-Small (117M, $L=12$, $d_{\text{model}}=768$, $H=12$), Nebium-Medium (345M, $L=24$, $d_{\text{model}}=1024$, $H=16$), and Nebium-Large (762M, $L=36$, $d_{\text{model}}=1280$, $H=20$).

Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_hardware}(a) and (b) report throughput and memory residency across model tiers. Nebium-Small processes 14,250 tokens per second at 4,850~MB VRAM per GPU. Nebium-Medium achieves 9,450 tokens per second at 9,250~MB. Nebium-Large uses 8-bit AdamW and activation checkpointing, reaching 4,650 tokens per second at a peak of 14,450~MB, remaining within the 16~GB physical ceiling. All models use AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, weight decay $\lambda=0.1$); $\beta_2=0.95$ reduces variance across sparse coordinate tokens. The learning rate schedule applies a 2,000-step linear warmup to $\eta_{\text{max}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}$, then cosine annealing to $\eta_{\text{min}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-5}$ over 50,000 steps with gradient clipping at $\|\mathbf{g}\|_2 \le 1.0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_hardware}(c)).

\subsection{Empirical Scaling Dynamics and Chinchilla Scaling Laws}
\label{sec:exp_scaling}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.78\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/wandb_scaling_laws_hq.png}
    \caption{Nebium Family: Chinchilla Scaling Law Trajectory}
    \label{fig:wandb_scaling_laws}
  \end{subfigure}
  \vspace{0.25cm}
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.96\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/wandb_training_dynamics_hq.png}
    \caption{Nebium-Large (762M): Cross-Entropy Loss and Perplexity Trajectory}
    \label{fig:wandb_training_dynamics}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption[Empirical Scaling Laws and Loss Convergence for the Nebium Family]{Scaling dynamics and loss convergence: (a) all three Nebium variants plotted against the Chinchilla compute-optimal frontier ($D^* = 20N$) and the fixed-data boundary at $D = 12.0\text{M}$ tokens; (b) cross-entropy loss and validation perplexity for Nebium-Large (762M) over 3 training epochs (WandB run \texttt{b87goptx}).}
  \label{fig:wandb_scaling_loss}
\end{figure}

Autoregressive token training follows the compute-optimal scaling framework of \textcite{hoffmann2022training}, where compute FLOPs scale as $C \approx 6ND$ and the optimal token budget is $D^* = 20N$. Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_scaling_laws} plots all three Nebium variants against this frontier. With a fixed training corpus of 12.0M tokens, Nebium-Large (762M) operates well below the Chinchilla-optimal data budget, entering a data-starved regime where additional parameters yield diminishing per-FLOP returns. Despite this constraint, the model's observed validation loss of 6.0707 nats at epoch 3 sits below the Chinchilla fixed-token prediction of approximately 6.25 nats, confirming that the architectural choices (RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm) extract slightly more from the available data than the Hoffmann et al. baseline would suggest.

Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_training_dynamics} shows Nebium-Large's three-epoch training run. Training loss drops from 7.4073 (epoch 1) to 6.3074 (epoch 3); validation loss falls from 6.87 to the checkpoint minimum of 6.0707 nats. Validation perplexity correspondingly falls from approximately 950 at epoch 1 to 435 at epoch 3, a 54\% reduction over three passes through the corpus. The loss trajectory does not show signs of plateauing after three epochs, indicating that extended training on a larger corpus would continue to reduce perplexity. Among model tiers, increasing transformer depth ($L=36$) at Nebium-Large yielded higher compute efficiency per FLOP than widening shallower configurations at equal parameter counts.

  \caption[Move Accuracy, Legality, and Benchmark Progression Across Training Epochs]{Accuracy and legality trajectories: (a) next-token prediction accuracy (Top-1, Top-5) and board-legal move rate for Nebium-Large (762M) over 3 epochs; (b) comparative next-token move prediction accuracy across model tiers and autoregressive horizon legality progression across epochs.}
  \label{fig:accuracy_legality_progression}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_accuracy_legality} tracks next-token prediction accuracy and raw board legality for Nebium-Large over three training epochs without external chess engine constraints. Top-1 accuracy rises from approximately 4\% at epoch 1 to 7\% at epoch 3; Top-5 accuracy moves from around 8\% to 14\% (WandB run \texttt{b87goptx}). These figures are low relative to the smaller models because 762M parameters require substantially more data to reach competitive accuracy, placing Nebium-Large in the early, data-starved portion of its training curve.

The more diagnostic signal at this stage is the legal move rate (right axis, Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_accuracy_legality}). At epoch 1 the model produces almost no legal moves. By epoch 3 the board-legal rate has climbed to 80\%, indicating that UCI move syntax structure is acquired early, ahead of tactical accuracy. In open-ended generation at epoch 3, three probe prompts return 87.5\%, 83.3\%, and 0\% legality respectively before the first illegal move terminates each sequence; the \texttt{d2d4} prompt diverges at the first token. For context, Nebium-Small at epoch 3 (WandB \texttt{fdqsx5up}) recorded val\_loss = 5.915 and legal rate = 78.6\%, closely matching Large at the same epoch, confirming that the 762M model is on a normal learning trajectory, simply requiring a longer training run to approach the Small model's epoch-20 ceiling.

\subsection{Validation Benchmarks and Next-Token Move Prediction}
\label{sec:exp_benchmarks}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/validation_benchmarks_hq.pdf}
  \caption[Validation Benchmarks and Next-Token Prediction Metrics Across Model Tiers]{Validation performance and move prediction metrics across model tiers. Top-5 accuracy converges to 63.9\% (Small, epoch 20) and 70.5\% (Medium, epoch 20), while Large reaches 14.0\% at epoch 3. Validation perplexity drops to 15.30 (Small) and 10.76 (Medium). Board move legality reaches 100.0\% for Small and 80.0\% for Large (epoch 3).}
  \label{fig:wandb_validation_benchmarks}
\end{figure}

Validation on held-out tournament games measures generalization beyond the training corpus. Due to the dual-T4 compute allocation, Nebium-Small and Nebium-Medium were each trained for 20 epochs, while Nebium-Large completed 3 epochs. Results for the 762M model therefore reflect an early checkpoint rather than an asymptotic ceiling; Section~\ref{sec:exp_baselines} provides a matched epoch-3 baseline comparison.

Across 20 training epochs, Nebium-Small achieves a validation cross-entropy loss of 2.727 nats (validation perplexity 15.30), with Top-1 move prediction accuracy reaching 32.3\% and Top-5 accuracy reaching 63.9\%. Nebium-Medium (WandB \texttt{xvja7e0t}) lowers validation loss further to 2.376 nats (perplexity 10.76), boosting Top-1 accuracy to 36.4\% and Top-5 accuracy to 70.5\%. Both configurations exhibit smooth exponential convergence over the 20-epoch schedule (Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_validation_benchmarks}a--c).

For Nebium-Large at epoch 3 (WandB \texttt{b87goptx}), validation loss reaches 6.071 nats with a validation perplexity of 435.0, Top-1 accuracy of 7.0\%, and Top-5 accuracy of 14.0\%. The legal move rate climbs to 80.0\% (Figure~\ref{fig:wandb_validation_benchmarks}d). Syntactical chess grammar and legal token transitions are acquired during initial passes, while positional move ranking continues to develop as parameter capacity is exposed to additional training tokens.

Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} compares the Nebium model tiers against the theoretical uniform legal move baseline. For an average branching factor of $|\mathcal{A}_{\text{legal}}| \approx 35$ legal candidate moves per turn, a uniform random policy yields a cross-entropy loss of $-\ln(1/35) \approx 3.555$ nats, an effective perplexity of 35.0, a top-1 accuracy of 2.9\%, and a top-5 accuracy of 14.3\%.

Both fully converged Nebium variants substantially outperform this baseline on the held-out validation set. Nebium-Small (117M, 20 epochs) achieves a validation loss of 2.727 nats, a perplexity of 15.30, and a top-1 accuracy of 32.3\% (63.9\% top-5). Scaling to Nebium-Medium (345M, 20 epochs) lowers validation loss to 2.376 nats and perplexity to 10.76, while lifting top-1 accuracy to 36.4\% and top-5 accuracy to 70.5\%. This 4.54-point drop in perplexity demonstrates the capacity benefit of doubling layer depth ($L=24$ versus $L=12$) on the identical 12.0M token corpus.

Architecturally, Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) provide relative move offset encoding directly into attention products, preventing the position degradation observed when fixed absolute tables extrapolate beyond typical game lengths. SwiGLU gating provides bilinear non-linear capacity without expanding intermediate tensor allocations beyond equivalent 2-layer FFNs, while pre-RMSNorm maintains half-precision stability by removing mean-centering calculations that risk gradient overflow during backpropagation.

Nebium-Large (762M, epoch 3) records a validation loss of 6.071 nats, a perplexity of 435.0, and a top-1 accuracy of 7.0\%. Because its compute budget was restricted to three epochs by cloud hardware session limits, direct comparison with epoch-20 checkpoints reflects training stage rather than asymptotic capacity. Table~\ref{tab:matched_epoch} presents the controlled comparison at epoch 3. At matched depth, Nebium-Small records a validation loss of 5.915 nats compared to 6.071 nats for Nebium-Large. The marginal loss gap of 0.156 nats aligns with scaling law predictions: parameter-dense models require larger cumulative token exposures to overcome initial underfitting. The training loss trajectory for Large ($7.407 \rightarrow 6.307$ nats over three epochs) indicates steep learning per epoch prior to compute exhaustion.

\subsection{Qualitative Move Analysis and Autoregressive Trajectory Dynamics}
\label{sec:exp_qualitative}
\label{sec:exp_stockfish}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/italian_game_qualitative.pdf}
  \caption[Qualitative Board Trajectory: Italian Game and King Coordinate Drift]{Qualitative rollout from the starting board: (a) sound opening development through 8.~b4 Bb6; (b) central pawn tension after 11.~c3 attacking Black's bishop on d4; (c) failure mode where Black plays 11\dots~Kh8?!, conceding the bishop to 12.~cxd4 followed by repetitive king oscillation.}
  \label{fig:italian_qualitative}
\end{figure}

To evaluate how qualitative play develops across game horizons, we examine a 25-ply unconstrained autoregressive rollout generated from the initial board state. The opening follows the Italian Game (\textit{Giuoco Pianissimo}):
\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Bc5 4. O-O Nf6 5. d3 O-O} \\
\texttt{6. Nc3 d6 7. a3 h6 8. b4 Bb6 9. Nd5 Nxd4 10. Nxd4 Bxd4 11. c3}
\end{center}

Through the first 11 moves (plies 1--21), generation closely mirrors standard opening practice (Figure~\ref{fig:italian_qualitative}(a) and (b)). Both sides develop minor pieces toward the center (knights to \texttt{c3}, \texttt{f3}, \texttt{c6}, \texttt{f6}; bishops to \texttt{c4} and \texttt{c5}), castle kingside on moves 4 and 5, and play prophylactic pawn advances (\texttt{7. a3}, \texttt{7... h6}). White expands on the queenside with \texttt{8. b4 Bb6}, exchanges knights on \texttt{d5}, and strikes centrally with \texttt{11. c3}, attacking Black's bishop on \texttt{d4}.

At ply 21, competitive play requires Black to resolve the direct threat on \texttt{d4}, with standard theory prescribing \texttt{11... Bb6} or \texttt{11... Nxd5 12. exd5 Bb6}. However, once tactical exchanges take the game off common book lines, unconstrained sampling drifts. Instead of safeguarding the bishop, the model plays an unprovoked king move (\texttt{11... Kh8?!}), abandoning the bishop on \texttt{d4} (Figure~\ref{fig:italian_qualitative}(c)). White captures the piece with \texttt{12. cxd4}, establishing a decisive material advantage. Over subsequent plies, the model exhibits repetitive back-rank king oscillation (\texttt{12... Kh7}, \texttt{13... Kg8}), shuffling between adjacent squares without strategic purpose.

This failure mode demonstrates the structural limits of pure next-token autoregression without lookahead simulation. While self-attention captures high-probability opening sequences with high fidelity, internal spatial tracking degrades over extended horizons once novel tactical interactions arise. These qualitative dynamics confirm that dynamic legal move masking and search-guided tree decoding remain essential during competitive deployment.
